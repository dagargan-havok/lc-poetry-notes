Elizabeth Bishop

Bishop is strongly interested in symbolism: the titled object, creature, or figure often points beyond itself. Her poems often combine close description with narrative and storytelling. She is interested in childhood experience, the natural world, and biblical stories or imagery. Some poems use complex speakers whose views may not fully match the poet's own. Biographical context: Bishop (1911–1979) lost her father at 8 months and her mother was institutionalised when she was 5. She grew up in Nova Scotia with grandparents, later lived in Brazil (c.1951–1967), and suffered from alcoholism throughout her life. Style hallmarks: precision of observation, emotional restraint and indirection, the outsider's viewpoint, and tonal shifts within a single poem.

Summary

The speaker arrives at a filthy filling station and reacts with shock and disgust. As she notices a doily, plant, taboret, and other domestic touches, her tone shifts from judgement to curiosity. The ending suggests an absent feminine presence, but the lecturer also questions whether the speaker ever fully gets past her dismissive attitude to the men.

Quotes & Analysis

"Oh, but it is dirty!"

A high-impact opening. The interjection throws us straight into the speaker's recoil.

"oil-soaked, oil-permeated"

Repetition doubles down on the grease and exaggerates the saturation of oil.

"Be careful with that match!"

An imperative command that highlights the danger of the place.

"Father wears a dirty, / oil-soaked monkey suit"

The lecturer explains "monkey suit" as work overalls, keeping the focus on labour and dirt.

"all quite thoroughly dirty"

Shows the speaker's fixation on grime and her sense of superiority.

"Do they live in the station?"

The lecturer says this sounds "a bit snobby."

"a big dim doily"

A surprising domestic object in a greasy setting.

"Why the extraneous plant? / Why the taboret? / Why, oh why, the doily?"

The tone shifts into curiosity about these homely items.

"Somebody embroidered the doily" / "Somebody waters the plant"

These details point to unseen domestic care.

"Somebody loves us all"

The lecturer reads this mainly as the absent mother or feminine touch. However, the line also works as a broader statement about love and care in unexpected places — both readings operate simultaneously.

Summary

The title invites comparison with the biblical Prodigal Son, but Bishop does not retell the story directly. Instead, the poem presents a degraded alcoholic in a pig pen and forces the reader to face the reality of shame, addiction, and disgust. The biblical foil makes the ending potentially hopeful, as the prodigal may return home and reintegrate positively.

Biographical context: Bishop herself suffered from alcoholism throughout her life. Critics widely read this poem as drawing on her own experience of addiction.

Form: The poem is a double sonnet — two stanzas of fourteen lines each. The tight, enclosed form mirrors the protagonist's trapped state.

Summary

Set in Brazil (where Bishop lived from c.1951–1967 with her partner Lota de Macedo Soares), the poem begins with the beauty of fire balloons (balões — paper lanterns released for Catholic saints' days) rising into the sky as part of an illegal but persistent religious tradition. It then turns sharply into panic and destruction when one falls. The suffering of the animals makes the poem a critique of traditions that continue even when they repeatedly cause harm.

The poem is dedicated to Robert Lowell, Bishop's close friend and fellow poet.

Themes

SymbolismTradition and harmBeauty turning to dangerSocial conscienceReligious traditionVulnerability and powerlessness

Quotes & Analysis

"This is the time of year / when almost every night / the frail, illegal fire balloons appear"

Establishes an annual tradition that continues despite being illegal.

"Climbing the mountain height, / rising toward a saint"

Shows that the practice is religiously inspired.

"Flush and fill with light / that comes and goes, like hearts"

The simile helps make the early scene vivid and engaging.

"Once up against the sky it's hard / to tell them from the stars"

The lecturer calls this language majestic and grandiose.

"receding, dwindling"

Present participles create energy and movement.

"suddenly turning dangerous"

Marks the point where the poem turns on its head.

"Last night another big one fell"

"Another" shows this is not the first time.

"splattered like an egg of fire against the cliff"

A striking simile of violent impact.

"the owls who nest there / shrieked up out of sight"

Highlights panic and the consequences for animals.

"a baby rabbit, dead in the road, / a handful of intangible ash / with fixed, ignited eyes"

The lecturer notes a play on the word "ignited."

"Too pretty, dreamlike mimicry!"

The balloons are a beautiful imitation of stars and planets, but only a false copy.

"a weak mailed fist / clenched ignorant against the sky"

Suggests frustrated, powerless protest.

Techniques

  • Strong tonal turn
  • Simile
  • Present participles
  • Rhyme and repeated sound for energy
  • Pun/play on words in "ignited"
  • Exclamation
  • Symbolism

Exam Points

  • The poem is not mainly about literal balloons or animals; it is about what they symbolize.
  • Bishop first presents the tradition as beautiful, then exposes its destructive effects.
  • "Another" is crucial because it shows repeated damage.
  • The lecturer's main emphasis is destructive tradition.
  • The poem has a clear social conscience.
  • The "weak mailed fist" captures anger without real power.

Summary

The speaker catches an old fish and describes it in a clear first-person narrative voice. As the details build up, the fish becomes a veteran survivor marked by earlier struggles. For the lecturer, the fish finally symbolizes the reverence we should feel for age and for everything an older being has overcome.

Themes

SymbolismSurvival and enduranceReverence for ageExperience and wisdomThe natural worldRespect

Quotes & Analysis

"I caught a tremendous fish"

A direct, story-like opening.

"He didn't fight. / He hadn't fought at all"

Part of the poem's clear, experiential narration.

"venerable"

Means old and respected.

"His brown skin hung in strips / like ancient wallpaper"

The simile conveys age and venerability.

"while his gills were breathing in / the terrible oxygen"

Oxygen is "terrible" for the fish because it needs water through its gills.

"I looked into his eyes" / "far larger than mine / but shallower"

The description shows the speaker's fascination and close attention.

"five old pieces of fish-line"

Reveals that the fish has survived earlier encounters.

"Like medals with their ribbons / frayed and wavering"

Turns survival into honour.

"a five-haired beard of wisdom"

Suggests wisdom, vitality, and experience.

"victory filled up / the little rented boat"

Marks the emotional climax of the encounter.

"rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!" / "And I let the fish go"

The poem ends with the fish's release.

Techniques

  • First-person experiential narrative
  • Detailed physical description
  • Simile
  • Metaphor
  • Symbolism
  • Practical, realistic fishing detail
  • Repetition at the ending

Exam Points

  • The fish becomes a symbol of reverence for age and overcome experience.
  • Bishop's practical fishing detail makes the poem convincing and vivid.
  • The hooks and lines show the fish as a survivor.
  • "Like medals..." and "beard of wisdom" turn survival into honour and respect.
  • The lecturer's emphasis is reverence for age and overcome experience.
  • The release matters because it follows the speaker's growing respect for the fish.

Summary

This poem presents a child's first encounter with death. The speaker looks at Arthur, the cold room, the chromographs, the stuffed loon, and the coffin, moving from one detail to another rather than fully facing death. The childlike comparisons and shifting attention show both incomprehension and a kind of insulation from the full force of grief.

Themes

Childhood perspectiveDeath and mortalityIncomprehensionMemoryImagination as protectionShifting attention

Quotes & Analysis

"In the cold, cold parlor"

Repetition emphasizes the coldness of the room and of death.

"beneath the chromographs"

The child's attention moves to the royal pictures as well as the body.

"a stuffed loon"

The loon becomes another focus of attention in the room.

"Since Uncle Arthur fired / a bullet into him, / he hadn't said a word"

A striking detail about the loon, given in a childlike, matter-of-fact way.

"His breast was deep and white, / cold and caressable"

The child notices physical detail rather than abstract grief.

"his eyes were red glass, / much to be desired"

Another unusual detail that catches the child's eye.

"Arthur's coffin was / a little frosted cake"

The lecturer links this directly to child perspective and limited understanding.

"Arthur was very small. / He was all white, like a doll"

A childlike comparison that makes the dead body more graspable to a child.

"Jack Frost had started to paint him"

A childlike explanation for Arthur's pallor.

"He had just begun on his hair, / a few red strokes"

Continues the Jack Frost image.

"the roads deep in snow"

The ending stays practical and childlike rather than fully abstract or philosophical.

Techniques

  • Child viewpoint
  • Repetition
  • Shifting focus
  • Childlike similes
  • Storytelling and memory
  • Juxtaposition of the dead boy and the dead loon
  • Mythic/childhood figure of Jack Frost

Exam Points

  • The poem shows a child trying to understand something too grave and complex to grasp fully.
  • The child's attention keeps shifting between Arthur, the loon, and the pictures on the wall.
  • Images like the frosted cake, doll, and Jack Frost make death seem more manageable to a child.
  • The lecturer suggests the child may focus on the loon because she cannot fully confront Arthur.
  • The speaker is partly insulated from grief by limited understanding.
  • The final focus on practical details, such as the snow-covered roads, shows the child's incomprehension.
