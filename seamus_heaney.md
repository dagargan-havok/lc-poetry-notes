# Seamus Heaney — Leaving Cert Poetry Notes

## Overview

- Heaney is strongly associated with craft and making. The lecturer repeatedly links physical craft and poetic craft.
- He is interested in history, identity, and narrative, and in how these overlap.
- His poems range from personal relationships and emotion (*A Call*) to national identity and landscape (*Bogland*) to childhood fear in the North (*A Constable Calls*).
- He often uses vivid physical detail and allusion to open up bigger meanings.
- In this lecture, the main allusive threads are Greek myth and the Hansel/Gretel story.

## Key Themes

- Craft and workmanship
- Identity: national, family, poetic, and childhood identity
- History and narrative
- Relationships and emotional connection
- Landscape and Ireland
- Fear, guilt, and authority
- Allusion and myth

## Poems

### The Forge

**Summary:** Brief summary of the poem

The lecturer presents *The Forge* as a poem about craft in two senses: the blacksmith's craft and the poet's craft. The forge is a place of energy and making, but the ending becomes bleaker, suggesting that these crafts are less valued than they once were and that the final action is "just work" rather than divinely inspired art.

**Themes:** List relevant themes

- Craft and making
- The poet and the blacksmith
- Traditional skills
- History and decline
- Human and divine creation

**Quotes & Analysis:**

- **"All I know is a door into the dark"** — the first-person opening and the alliteration of **"door"** and **"dark"** create energy and draw the reader towards the forge.
- **"Outside, old axles and iron hoops rusting"** — when the gaze turns away from the forge, the lecturer hears a calmer effect through assonance.
- **"Inside, the hammered anvil's short-pitched ring"** — turning back towards the forge restores momentum. The lecturer calls **"short-pitched ring"** an example of **sprung rhythm**, helping to convey the rise and fall of the hammer.
- **"The unpredictable fantail of sparks"** — vivid visual imagery makes the forge feel exciting and alive.
- **"Or hiss when a new shoe toughens in water"** — the lecturer identifies **"hiss"** as onomatopoeia.
- **"The anvil must be somewhere in the centre ... / Set there immoveable: an altar"** — the anvil is the centre of the place, and **"altar"** gives the forge a religious dimension.
- **"Horned as a unicorn"** — this gives the anvil a mythical quality.
- **"Sometimes, leather-aproned, hairs in his nose"** — this detail demystifies the craftsman; the poem starts to bring him down from the earlier elevated level.
- **"He recalls a clatter of hoofs / Where traffic is flashing in rows"** — the lecturer links this to an older world being replaced by modern traffic; the craft is not as in demand as it once was.
- **"Then grunts and goes in, with a slam and flick / To beat real iron out, to work the bellows"** — the ending is not triumphant. The lecturer reads it as a fall from the earlier near-divine status: now it is simply work. *(Note: many standard LC commentaries read this ending more positively, as the blacksmith's continued devotion to his craft despite the modern world. The lecturer's bleaker reading is one valid interpretation — be prepared to argue either in an exam.)*

**Techniques:**

- Sonnet form linked to traditional craft
- Alliteration
- Assonance
- Sprung rhythm
- Visual imagery
- Onomatopoeia
- Religious imagery
- Mythical imagery

**Exam Points:**

- The lecturer says the blacksmith and the poet overlap throughout the poem.
- The forge is energetic and exciting when the poem turns towards it.
- **"altar"** and **"unicorn"** briefly elevate the craftsman towards the divine.
- The last lines are bleaker: the craft may have "had their day."
- The poem is useful for discussing craft, tradition, and the changing value of skilled work.

### The Underground

**Summary:** Brief summary of the poem

The lecturer reads *The Underground* as a more narrative poem with two levels: a literal journey through the London Underground with Heaney's wife, and an allusive underworld journey shaped by older stories. The opening is energetic and playful, the return journey darkens, and the ending is hopeful because Heaney breaks away from tragic myth.

**Themes:** List relevant themes

- Love and marriage
- Journey and movement
- Narrative allusion
- Greek myth and Hansel/Gretel
- Time and continuity
- Hope

**Quotes & Analysis:**

- **"There we were in the vaulted tunnel running"** — the poem opens with characters and action straight away, creating energy.
- **"You in your going-away coat speeding ahead"** — the lecturer highlights the sequence of present participles such as **"running," "speeding,"** and **"gaining"** to show motion. The "going-away coat" is the traditional outfit a bride wears when leaving the wedding — the poem is set on Heaney's honeymoon with Marie in London.
- **"me then like a fleet god gaining / Upon you before you turned to a reed"** — this opening allusion references Pan chasing the nymph Syrinx (from Ovid's *Metamorphoses*), who turned into a reed to escape him. This is the first of three mythological allusions in the poem.
- **"Button after button sprang off and fell in a trail / Between the Underground and the Albert Hall"** — literally, the buttons fall as they rush along; later, the trail becomes important in the Hansel/Gretel allusion.
- **"Late for the Proms"** — the lecturer links the early part of the poem to fun, movement, and the carefree mood of newlyweds.
- **"Our echoes die in that corridor"** — the lecturer points to this as a shift in tone after the earlier playfulness.
- **"and now I come as Hansel came / on the moonlit stones retracing the path back"** — the Hansel and Gretel allusion grows out of the trail of buttons as the speaker retraces his steps after the Proms.
- **"damned if I look back"** — this is linked directly to Orpheus and Eurydice. Like Orpheus, the speaker is moving ahead with his wife behind him, but he refuses the fatal backward look.
- The lecturer says the poem uses these allusions to connect experience across time and to show how narrative can "collapse time."

**Techniques:**

- Narrative voice
- Energetic opening verbs
- Allusion to Hansel and Gretel
- Allusion to Greek myth
- Tonal shift
- Repeated narrative echoes across time

**Exam Points:**

- The lecturer says there are "basically two narratives going on": the Tube journey and the underworld reading.
- The opening is full of energy and movement.
- Hansel/Gretel and Orpheus/Eurydice are the key allusions to know. The Pan/Syrinx opening makes three mythological layers in total.
- The poem is a celebration of narrative and of how stories connect people across time.
- The ending is hopeful because, unlike Orpheus, Heaney breaks from myth rather than repeating its tragedy.

### A Call

**Summary:** Brief summary of the poem

The lecturer treats *A Call* as a very experiential, personal poem. A woman answers the phone and says she will go and get Heaney's father. While waiting, the speaker imagines his father in the garden. The pause becomes charged with time, mortality, and a yearning for a deeper emotional connection that is still not fully spoken.

**Themes:** List relevant themes

- Family relationship
- Emotional difficulty
- Connection and distance
- Time and mortality
- Craft and manual work

**Quotes & Analysis:**

- **"Hold on. / She said, I'll just run out and get him."** — the opening dialogue is the hook. The lecturer stresses how conversational and immediate it is.
- **"I saw him / down on his hands and knees beside the leek rig"** — the speaker imagines his father in his mind's eye while waiting for the call to connect. (A "leek rig" is a garden bed for growing leeks.)
- **"touching, inspecting, separating one stock from the other"** — the lecturer connects this careful gardening to Heaney's own craft as a poet: both involve precision and selection.
- **"Then found myself listening to the amplified grave-ticking of hall clocks"** — **"grave-ticking"** is explained as a pun: grave means solemn, but it also suggests burial and death.
- **"where the phone lay unattended in a cam of mirror glass and sun-struck pendulums"** — the unattended phone and pendulums strengthen the sense that time is passing while the speaker waits.
- **"this is how death would summon every man"** — the lecturer reads Death here as the Grim Reaper, which deepens the mortality theme.
- **"Next thing he spoke"** — the literal connection finally happens.
- **"nearly said, I loved him. Nearly."** — this is crucial for the lecturer: the connection is still incomplete, and the deepest feeling remains almost said rather than said.

**Techniques:**

- Dialogue as an opening hook
- First-person experiential voice
- Concrete domestic detail
- Craft imagery through gardening
- Pun
- Mortality imagery

**Exam Points:**

- The poem is especially useful for questions on relationships and emotional difficulty.
- The speaker's waiting creates the poem's reflective space.
- The father's gardening is described in a way the lecturer links to Heaney's own craft.
- **"grave-ticking"** is the key phrase for time and death.
- **"Nearly"** matters: the poem ends with yearning rather than full emotional resolution.

### Bogland

**Summary:** Brief summary of the poem

The lecturer reads *Bogland* as a poem of Irish landscape and identity. The bog can stand for Ireland itself. By contrasting bogland with prairies and by stressing how the bog preserves old life, the poem turns landscape into history, memory, and national meaning.

**Themes:** List relevant themes

- Ireland and national identity
- Landscape
- Preservation
- History
- Depth and mystery

**Quotes & Analysis:**

- **"We have no prairies / To slice a big sun at evening"** — the opening defines Ireland by contrast with America. The lecturer notes the vivid imagery and the small alliterative force of **"slice"** and **"sun."**
- **"Everywhere the eye concedes to encroaching horizon / is wooed into the cyclops' eye of a tern"** — the lecturer says this gives grandeur to the landscape and links it to myth.
- **"Our unfenced country / Is bog"** — the bog can stand for Ireland, and the lecturer suggests **"unfenced"** sounds positive.
- **"They've taken the skeleton / Of the Great Irish Elk / Out of the peat"** — the bog preserves ancient life and gives the landscape a near-mythic scale.
- **"Butter sunk under / More than a hundred years / Was recovered salty and white"** — this is another example of the bog's preserving nature.
- **"The ground itself is kind, black butter / Melting and opening underfoot"** — the lecturer reads this positively and repeatedly stresses Heaney's intimate physical knowledge of bogland.
- **"Missing its last definition / By millions of years"** — the bog is still not finally defined.
- **"They'll never dig coal here"** — again, the poem defines Ireland partly by what it is not.
- **"Every layer they strip / Seems camped on before"** — each layer suggests earlier life and earlier presence.
- **"The bogholes might be Atlantic seepage"** — the lecturer is tentative here and says he is "not 100% sure" about this line, so any reading should stay cautious.
- **"The wet centre is bottomless"** — this has literal depth, but the lecturer also says it can suggest depth of meaning, national identity, and our place in history.

**Techniques:**

- Contrast
- Vivid imagery
- Alliteration
- Mythical allusion
- Physical detail
- Metaphor

**Exam Points:**

- The lecturer says *Bogland* can refer to Ireland, not just to a bog.
- The prairie comparison helps define Irish identity by difference.
- The bog preserves old life, objects, and history.
- Heaney's physical detail shows closeness to the place he describes.
- The final bottomlessness can be read literally and symbolically.

### A Constable Calls

**Summary:** Brief summary of the poem

The lecturer reads *Constable Calls* as a darker, more political, North-specific poem. From a child's perspective, the constable's visit fills the house with fear. The child notices the bicycle, cap, ledger, holster, and other tools of authority more than the man himself. The father's lie and the final ticking bicycle build guilt and tension.

**Themes:** List relevant themes

- Childhood fear
- Authority and the law
- Tension and guilt
- Northern political atmosphere
- Violence in the background

**Quotes & Analysis:**

- **"His bicycle stood at the windowsill"** — the lecturer notes that the constable's presence is felt before he is properly named in the body of the poem.
- **"The rubber cowl of a mud-splasher / skirting the front mudguard"** — the detailed focus on the bicycle suggests the child may be looking away from the man himself.
- **"His cap was upside down on the floor"** — again, attention stays on objects around the constable rather than on the constable directly.
- **"Arithmetic and fear"** — official counting and record-keeping are tied directly to anxiety.
- **"But was there not a line of turnips where the seed ran out in the potato field?"** — the lecturer treats this as the father's lie, which increases the child's fear.
- **"the black hole in the barracks"** — the child imagines punishment if the lie is discovered.
- **"A shadow bobbed in the window"** — the constable remains shadowy and threatening.
- **"his boot pushed off / and the bicycle ticked"** — the lecturer says the ticking raises tension with every beat and can also suggest bombs and violence in the wider Northern context.

**Techniques:**

- Child perspective
- Object detail
- Symbols and tools of law
- Accumulation of fear
- Repetition in the ticking
- Political tension beneath a domestic scene

**Exam Points:**

- The lecturer calls this poem more political and more North-specific than the others in the set.
- Fear is conveyed through the objects and symbols of authority.
- The father's lie introduces guilt as well as fear.
- The ticking bicycle intensifies tension and opens the poem out towards wider violence.
- This is a strong poem for discussing social issues, childhood fear, tension, and guilt.
